A fleet can order electric trucks or school buses and take delivery within months. Getting the utility to power the chargers takes much longer. In California's most recent filings (30 September 2026), the average electric vehicle charging project took **564 calendar days** from application to power at PG&E. At SCE it took **578 business days**, roughly 2.3 years of working days. These are the projects that finished. Projects that needed a new circuit or a substation upgrade waited longer still. This report puts the regulator targets, the utilities' own reported timelines and the equipment lead times behind them side by side.

## The short answer

| Step or scenario | Typical wait | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CPUC target, EV charging service (Rule 29/45), utility-controlled steps | 182 days average, 335 days maximum | CPUC D.24-09-020, Sept 2024 |
| PG&E, EV charging projects, application to energization | 564 calendar days average (237 projects) | PG&E filing, 30 Sept 2026 |
| SCE, EV charging projects, application to energization | 578 business days average; median 541 | SCE filing, 30 Sept 2026 |
| SDG&E, EV charging projects, final submittal to transformer set | 347 business days average (34 projects) | SDG&E filing, 30 Sept 2026 |
| PG&E circuit upgrade / substation upgrade | 942 / 1,285 calendar days | PG&E filing, 30 Sept 2026 |
| CPUC maximum for a new substation | 3,242 days (8.9 years) | CPUC D.24-09-020 |
| New York, transportation electrification projects | "a few months upstate, a year or longer downstate" (stakeholders) | NY DPS staff, 15 June 2026 |
| Utility construction for school-bus charging | nine months to two years (one utility) | EPA OIG 24-P-0012, Dec 2023 |
| Large power transformer | 2–4 years, vs months in 2020 | NIAC, June 2024 |

| Switchgear | 44 weeks average (Q2 2025) | Wood Mackenzie, Sept 2025 |

Plan on 18 months to two years for a new medium-sized depot connection in California. Plan on three years or more if the utility says the circuit or substation must be upgraded.

## California's energization targets

California has binding, published targets for how fast its large utilities must connect new load. The CPUC adopted them in Decision 24-09-020 on 12 September 2024, under the Powering Up Californians Act (SB 410). The clock starts when the customer requests energization.

| Request type | Average target (calendar days) | Maximum target (calendar days) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rule 15 (distribution line extension) | 182 | 357 |
| Rule 16 (service extension) | 182 | 335 |
| Rule 15/16 combined | 182 | 306 |
| **Rule 29/45 (EV charging infrastructure)** | **182** | **335** |
| Application decision | 10 | 45 |

| Upstream capacity upgrade | Maximum timeline (calendar days) |
| --- | --- |
| New or upgraded circuit | 684 (1.9 years) |
| Substation upgrade | 1,021 (2.8 years) |
| New substation | 3,242 (8.9 years) |

Rule 29 is the EV infrastructure tariff at PG&E and SCE; Rule 45 is SDG&E's. SCE converts the targets to working days: 125 business days average and 230 maximum for Rule 29/45.

The catch is in what the targets count. PG&E's filings state that the average targets cover only "the steps of the energization processes that are fully within its control." Customer design work, permits, inspections and third-party steps sit outside the clock. A utility can meet its target while the site still waits well over a year.

## What PG&E, SCE and SDG&E actually report

Each utility files a biannual energization report in CPUC docket R.24-01-018. The most recent set was filed on 30 September 2026 and covers applications from 31 January 2023 to 30 June 2026.

### PG&E: end-to-end time keeps rising

| PG&E filing | Applications covered | EV projects energized | PG&E-controlled days | End-to-end days | Share under maximum target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| June 2025 (SB 410 assessment) | Jan 2023 – Dec 2024 | 56 | 120 | 467 | 96.4% |
| 30 Sept 2025 | Jan 2023 – Jun 2025 | 86 | 113 | 508 | 97.7% |
| 1 Apr 2026 | Jan 2023 – Dec 2025 | 161 | 111 | 560 | 96.5% |
| 30 Sept 2026 | Jan 2023 – Jun 2026 | 237 | 81 | 564 | 99% |

PG&E's own share of the work fell from about 120 days to 81 days. Over the same period the end-to-end time rose from 467 to 564 days. PG&E now meets its maximum target on 99% of EV projects, while the average project takes more than a year and a half. The September 2026 averages draw on the 55% of applications already energized. Projects still waiting are not in the average.

### SCE: more than two years of working days

| SCE filing | Measure | EV charging (Rule 29/45) | EV charging plus line extension (Rule 29/45 & 15) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mar 2025 (as restated Sept 2025) | Steps 1–8, calendar days | 523 (360 business days) | 527 |
| 30 Sept 2025 | Steps 1–8, calendar days | 563 (387 business days) | 605 |
| 1 Apr 2026 | From application, business days | 571 average; 518 median | 605 average; 638 median |
| 30 Sept 2026 | From application, business days | 578 average; 541 median | 673 average; 681 median |

SCE changed its method in 2026. The first two rows count the eight energization steps in calendar days. The last two use the statutory table of average, median and standard deviation in business days from receipt of an application. Compare within each method, not across. Both show the same direction: longer.

SCE's September 2025 filing shows where the time goes. It received 421 Rule 29 applications between January 2023 and June 2025. Of those, 70 were energized, 52 were cancelled and 299 were still in progress. On completed projects, utility-controlled steps averaged 338 calendar days and customer-controlled steps 522. Some steps run in parallel, so the parts add up to more than the total. SCE also says its utility figures still include customer time it can't yet separate, and that upstream capacity work sits inside its engineering step.

### SDG&E: about 16 months, few projects

| SDG&E filing | EV charging (Rule 45), business days | Projects | EV charging plus line extension (Rule 15/45), business days | Projects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30 Sept 2025 | 362 | 17 | 324 | 7 |
| 1 Apr 2026 | 370 | 28 | 348 | 14 |
| 30 Sept 2026 | 347 | 34 | 344 | 16 |

SDG&E measures from the applicant's final submittal to the day the transformer is set, when the job is "ready for service." 347 business days is about 16 months. SDG&E's pre- and post-decision comparison shows no Rule 45 average yet for applications made after the September 2024 decision. In other words, the faster post-decision process has not yet shown up for EV charging jobs in its data.

## When the circuit or substation needs an upgrade

The figures above are mostly sites the existing grid could serve with a new service drop and transformer. Bigger fleet depots often need more: a new feeder, a reconductored line or a larger substation transformer. That is where the wait runs into years.

| PG&E filing | Circuit (new or upgraded), calendar days | Substation upgrade, calendar days | New substation | Projects needing upstream work | Days from need identified to energization |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30 Sept 2025 | 871 | 1,225 | None completed | Not reported | Not reported |
| 1 Apr 2026 | 950 | 1,285 | None completed | 23 | 492 |
| 30 Sept 2026 | 942 | 1,285 | None completed | 62 | 433 |

A PG&E substation upgrade averages 1,285 days, about 3.5 years. That's 26% longer than the CPUC's 1,021-day maximum. Circuit work runs 942 days against a 684-day maximum. PG&E says the project-level wait is shorter (433 days on average from when a project's upgrade need was flagged) because some upgrades serve several customers or are already underway when an application arrives. PG&E has not completed a new substation in any reporting period since 2023. SDG&E's September 2026 filing lists four upstream upgrades triggered by open requests and two more on closed jobs.

New York shows the same pattern. NYSEG and RG&E petitioned the Public Service Commission in November 2024 for substation projects with in-service dates from 2028 to 2031. Every NYSEG project listed "Possible School Bus Charging" as a driver. At one RG&E substation, school-bus charging made up 63% of the load requested. Greece Central School District near Rochester needs about 5 MW for 190 electric buses; its local substation had about 1.5 MW to spare in January 2025.

## New York: no targets, and a year or more downstate

New York has no energization targets and no public request-to-energization data. The utilities' joint EV application queue manual (updated December 2025) promises applicants only "an estimated timeframe."

The clearest official record is a New York Department of Public Service staff report dated 15 June 2026, "Recommendations for Addressing Electric Vehicle Charger Load Interconnection Issues." It summarizes what stakeholders told staff:

- Transportation electrification projects often take longer than expected: "a few months upstate, a year or longer downstate."
- "EV fleets may be delivered in a few months," but upgrades and interconnection approvals "often take six months or longer."
- Some projects have sat with construction complete "for many months before energization."
- Load studies take up to 20 weeks, and funds can't be committed until they finish.
- Dense urban projects above 3 MW can require a utility "blockhouse."

Stakeholders asked for end-to-end timeline tracking from application to energization. They also asked for a public dashboard, separate reporting for projects under and over 300 kW, and flexible connections. DPS staff routed these to existing proceedings. Large-load interconnection goes to Case 26-E-0045, opened on 12 February 2026, with a technical conference due before 31 December 2026. A flexible-interconnection pilot proposed by National Grid (filed 1 January 2026) is under staff review.

On the customer side, NYSERDA's site-planning guidance for school districts allows 16–20 months for switchgear and 12–18 months for construction. Federal auditors heard similar numbers. In EPA OIG report 24-P-0012 (27 December 2023), one utility said construction for school-bus charging could take nine months to two years. The same report cites a 12–24-month process to build and connect charging stations.

For the full New York picture, see [electric school buses in New York](/electric-school-buses/new-york) and [New York's electric school bus implementation gap](/research/ny-implementation-gap).

## Transformer and switchgear lead times

Even with a quick utility, equipment can set the pace. The transformers and switchgear a charging site needs are in short supply.

| Equipment | Lead time | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transformers ordered by utilities or developers | 2–4 years, vs months in 2020; one US plant quoted 5 years | NIAC, June 2024 |
| Transformers, average (Wood Mackenzie) | ~50 weeks (2021) to 120 weeks (2024) | NIAC, June 2024 |
| Large substation and generator step-up transformers | 80–210 weeks | NIAC, June 2024 |
| Power transformers, average | 128 weeks (Q2 2025, down 10 weeks on Q1) | Wood Mackenzie, Sept 2025 |
| Generator step-up transformers | 143 weeks (Q2 2025) | Wood Mackenzie, Sept 2025 |
| Distribution transformers | +429% average lead time 2020–2022; some utilities over 3 years | APPA survey of 95 utilities, via ATE, 2023 |
| 1,000 kVA distribution transformer | "up to two years and even longer" | ATE, 2023 |
| Switchgear | 3 months to 12 months; prices up 4 to 10 times | ATE, 2023 |
| Switchgear | 44 weeks average (Q2 2025) | Wood Mackenzie, Sept 2025 |
| Switchgear for school-bus depots | 16–20 months | NYSERDA, July 2024 |

Three points matter for fleet sites:

- **The distribution transformer is the depot's own bottleneck.** The APPA survey found that one in five utility projects was deferred or cancelled because of the distribution transformer shortage.
- **Substation transformers set the multi-year waits.** At 128 weeks on average, a substation transformer alone takes about 2.5 years to arrive, before installation. This is why PG&E's substation upgrades average 3.5 years.
- **The customer's switchgear has its own lead time.** It runs in parallel with the utility's work. If it arrives late, it holds up energization even when the utility is ready.

## How fleets can shorten the wait

The filings and the NY record point to the same practical steps:

1. **Apply before you buy vehicles.** End-to-end averages of 18–24 months mean a charging application should go in before the vehicle order, not after delivery.
2. **Ask early whether upstream work is needed.** That single answer separates a roughly 18-month project from a three-year one. SCE's filing points customers to the Available Load Capacity layers in its DRPEP map portal for a first look.
3. **Order long-lead equipment yourself and early.** Switchgear averages 44 weeks, and NYSERDA tells school districts to allow 16–20 months. Order it as soon as the design is fixed.
4. **Ask for a phased or flexible connection.** New York stakeholders asked for flexible connections with static, scheduled or dynamic load limits, and National Grid has a pilot proposal under review. A partial connection can carry a fleet until the full upgrade arrives.
5. **Keep customer steps short.** SCE's data shows customer-controlled steps averaging longer than its own. Permits, design approvals and payments are the part a fleet controls.
