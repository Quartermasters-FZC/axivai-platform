The New York School Bus Incentive Program (NYSBIP) is the state's main source of money for zero-emission school buses. NYSERDA runs it with $500 million from the 2022 Environmental Bond Act. The program pays two kinds of voucher: a **school bus voucher**, which the dealer takes off the invoice, and a **charging voucher**, which reimburses the fleet after chargers are installed. This guide sets out the amounts, the process, what has been awarded so far, and where projects get stuck.

## NYSBIP at a glance

- **Funding:** $500 million of the $4.2 billion Clean Water, Clean Air and Green Jobs Environmental Bond Act, which voters approved in 2022. It was released in three tranches: $100 million (September 2023), $200 million (August 2024) and $200 million (July 2025, the "third installment").
- **Who can apply:** New York public school districts, other public bodies that transport pupils, and third-party operators under contract with a district. BOCES can also apply for shared chargers.
- **Eligible buses:** Battery-electric and hydrogen fuel cell school buses on NYSERDA's Eligible ESB List, either new or repowered.
- **How money is allocated:** First come, first served, within per-district caps. Bus vouchers opened on 29 November 2023 and charging vouchers on 5 March 2024. NYSERDA's dataset shows the first bus project approved on 24 February 2024.
- **Separate state money:** The FY2026 budget (signed 9 May 2025) added up to $100 million through the Sustainable Future Program. That money is in addition to the Bond Act's $500 million, not part of it.

**The mandate has moved.** The 2026–27 state budget pushed New York's zero-emission school bus deadlines back by five years. Education Law §3638, as revised on 29 May 2026, now requires:

- every school bus bought or leased to be zero-emission from **1 July 2032** (previously 2027);
- every bus in operation to be zero-emission by **1 July 2040** (previously 2035).

Districts can still get up to two extensions of up to 24 months each. A second extension requires that the district has at least engaged with NYSERDA on a fleet electrification plan. Guides that still cite 2027 and 2035 are out of date. The voucher money did not change: NYSERDA still lists $500 million, and its program page now gives the 2032 and 2040 dates.

## School bus voucher amounts

The base voucher covers 60% of the extra cost of a new electric bus over a diesel one, or 75% for a repowered bus. Bonuses and add-ons are added on top. Amounts are per bus.

| Bus type | Base voucher | Priority District bonus | Scrappage bonus | V2G add-on | Wheelchair add-on | Maximum per bus |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New Type A | $114,000 | $28,500 | $47,500 | $9,500 | $8,000 | $207,500 |
| New Type C | $147,000 | $36,750 | $61,250 | $12,250 | $8,000 | $265,250 |
| New Type D | $156,000 | $39,000 | $65,000 | $13,000 | $8,000 | $281,000 |
| Repowered Type A | $105,000 | $21,000 | n/a | $7,000 | n/a | $133,000 |
| Repowered Type C | $135,000 | $27,000 | n/a | $9,000 | n/a | $171,000 |

*Source: NYSERDA NYSBIP program page and Implementation Manual (v5, December 2024). The maximum is the sum of every bonus and add-on. The most common award in NYSERDA's project data is $257,250: a new Type C bus with the priority, scrappage and V2G amounts (118 of 461 buses).*

**Who counts as a Priority District.** The priority bonus goes to districts the State Education Department classes as High Need/Resource Capacity, and to districts serving Disadvantaged Communities (DACs) under the Climate Justice Working Group criteria. Buses kept at a depot inside a DAC also qualify. NYSERDA's published list (January 2024) names 377 districts plus every New York City district. The Bond Act requires that DACs get at least 35% of the benefits of total Bond Act funds, with a goal of 40%, and NYSERDA aims for 40% within NYSBIP.

**Scrappage rules.** To get the scrappage bonus, the district must destroy the bus being replaced: a three-inch hole cut in the engine block and the frame rails cut through. The old bus must also meet every one of these tests:

- it is an internal combustion bus with an engine at least seven model years older than the application year;
- it ran in the district in the past 12 months, and in New York for 24 of the past 36 months;
- it covered at least 2,500 miles in the past 12 months, unless the engine is at least 10 years old or the odometer reads over 110,000 miles;
- it is being replaced by a bus of the same type or a smaller one.

## Charging vouchers and fleet electrification plans

Charging vouchers are paid per electric bus. A district can claim one for a bus funded by NYSBIP, by EPA or by itself, as long as the bus was bought on or after 1 January 2023.

| | Base charging voucher | With a Fleet Electrification Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Non-priority district | $25,000 per bus | $55,000 per bus |
| Priority District | $35,000 per bus | $65,000 per bus |

*Source: NYSERDA NYSBIP program page; Implementation Manual, Table 8.*

- **Eligible costs:** charger hardware, installation (trenching, pads, repaving), customer-side make-ready work (switchgear, panels, wiring, conduit) and battery storage connected to a charger.
- **Not eligible:** utility-side make-ready work, networking, extended warranties, permitting fees, project management and solar.
- **Ports:** up to one port per electric bus, plus one extra if the count is odd and the chargers are dual-port. Any mix of Level 2 and DC fast chargers is allowed.
- **Limit:** each purchaser was capped at $2 million in charging vouchers until 1 December 2025.
- **BOCES:** a BOCES can claim up to 10 ports of 60 kW or more for visiting district buses, without buying a bus, if it completes a Modified FEP.

**The Fleet Electrification Plan (FEP)** adds $30,000 per bus to the charging voucher and raises the district's bus cap. NYSERDA pays 100% of FEP costs for priority districts and districts whose depot is in a DAC, and 75% for everyone else. By July 2025, 401 districts (about 57% of the state) and 13 BOCES were working on an FEP, with 37 consulting firms involved. NYSERDA expected about 200 plans to be finished by the end of 2025.

## How to apply, and the dealer's role

**School bus vouchers go through the dealer.** The district does not apply for these itself.

1. Choose a bus and an approved dealer from the NYSBIP Eligible ESB List.
2. Sign the Purchaser Participation Agreement and the Fleet Certification. A Third-Party Operator Addendum is also needed if a contractor will run the bus.
3. Issue a purchase order. The dealer then files the School Bus Voucher Application in the NYSERDA Portal.
4. Within 4 months of approval, the dealer must upload any award letters from other programs. Within 9 months, it must upload the final purchase order, with the voucher shown as a deduction.
5. Take delivery and register the bus. The dealer then redeems the voucher within 18 months of approval. NYSERDA can extend this to a maximum of 24 months, and pays the dealer within about 30 days.

Eleven dealers hold NYSBIP projects. The two largest, JP Bus & Truck Repair and Navistar, account for 237 of the 461 buses.

**Charging vouchers go straight to the fleet.** On 29 January 2026, the Charging Voucher Portal moved to NYSERDA's partner portal. New applicants must first have a contractor application approved. After that:

- charger details are due within 60 days of pre-approval;
- a pending FEP must be uploaded within 180 days of pre-approval;
- the voucher must be redeemed within 15 months of approval, once chargers are installed, working and paid for. NYSERDA can grant up to 12 more months.

**Caps.** Until 1 December 2025, these limits applied:

- non-priority districts could claim the greater of 6 buses or 6% of their fleet;
- priority districts could claim the greater of 10 buses or 10% of their fleet;
- an approved FEP added the greater of 4 buses or 4%;
- no district could take more than 25% of program funds, and New York City counts as one district.

The March 2026 manual rewrote the caps on a fleet basis: per district statewide, and per contractor in New York City.

**Operating obligations.** Each bus must run at least 5,000 miles a year for five years, and the district files an annual vehicle usage report. NYSERDA can require telematics, at its own expense.

## What has been awarded so far

NYSERDA publishes every approved and closed bus voucher on data.ny.gov. AXIVAI analysed the release dated 26 October 2026.

| Measure | Value (26 Oct 2026) |
|---|---|
| Buses with an approved or closed voucher | 461 |
| Voucher value | $83.3 million |
| School districts and BOCES | 79 (77 districts, 2 BOCES) |
| Closed (bus delivered, voucher paid) | 177 buses, $31.1 million, 47 districts |
| Approved, not yet closed | 284 buses, $52.2 million, 42 districts |
| Buses for Priority Districts | 322 (70%), $57.5 million |
| New York City | 126 buses, $16.9 million |
| Approved in 2024 / 2025 / 2026 | 255 / 158 / 48 buses |
| Battery-electric / hydrogen fuel cell | 456 / 5 |
| Median approval-to-close time | 542 days (about 18 months) |

*Source: NYSERDA, NYSBIP School Bus Projects: Beginning 2023 (data.ny.gov), AXIVAI analysis. District names were de-duplicated where the same district appears under two spellings.*

Two things stand out:

- **Spending is slow.** About $83 million of the $500 million is committed to buses, and only $31 million has been paid out. Charging vouchers come on top of that, but NYSERDA publishes no project-level charging data.
- **Approvals are slowing.** New bus approvals fell from 255 buses in 2024 to 48 in 2026 so far.

In July 2025, NYSERDA reported that 88 districts had applied for 529 buses, 406 of them for 50 priority districts. The lower figures today reflect applications that were cancelled or never approved. Those applications were never purchases.

Some districts are larger than others: New York City, Buffalo (25 buses), Syracuse (22), Evans-Brant (14) and Albany (12) lead the list. For district-level detail, see the [New York state page](/electric-school-buses/new-york).

## Stacking with EPA, utility make-ready and tax credits

NYSBIP may be combined with other public incentives, but the total cannot exceed 100% of the cost of the bus or the chargers. Fleets must report every other award to NYSERDA.

**EPA Clean School Bus.** A bus funded by EPA can also get a NYSBIP *stacking voucher*. The amount is $50,000 for an EPA priority district or $100,000 for an EPA non-priority district, for Type A, C or D buses. Purchasers who take it give up the NYSBIP priority, scrappage and add-on bonuses. Stacking vouchers count toward the district's bus cap.

The federal side has largely stopped:

- EPA cancelled the 2024 rebate round in February 2026 and asked applicants to reapply under a revamped program.
- That program's request for information names propane, natural gas, hydrogen and biofuels.
- As of 26 October 2026, EPA lists no open funding opportunity. The program had $2.37 billion left at the start of FY2026.
- Nationally, EPA had awarded $2.62 billion to 1,143 districts for 8,223 buses before the pause.

**Utility make-ready.** The Joint Utilities' Medium- and Heavy-Duty Make-Ready Pilot can be stacked with NYSBIP. It covers up to 90% of utility-side costs at every site. On the customer side:

- sites in a DAC get up to 90% of the utility's per-kW cap;
- sites within two miles of a DAC get up to 50%;
- private depots outside those areas get no customer-side incentive.

The per-kW caps range from $409.07 (NYSEG) to $980.76 (Con Edison). A January 2026 PSC order extended the pilot to shared charging hubs. The pilot runs until its funds are used up or a permanent program replaces it.

**Federal tax credits are gone.** The 45W commercial clean vehicle credit ended for vehicles acquired after 30 September 2025. The 30C charger credit ended for property placed in service after 30 June 2026. Older funding guides that add "$40,000 per bus" from 45W are wrong for any purchase made now.

## Common pitfalls

**1. The bus arrives before the power.** NYSBIP does not require chargers or grid service before delivery. The dealer is paid once the bus is delivered and registered. The grid side is much slower:

- New York DPS staff reported in June 2026 that stakeholders describe grid work taking "a few months upstate, a year or longer downstate". Some sites sat fully built for months waiting to be energized.
- Large power transformers now take two to four years, against months in 2020 (NIAC, June 2024). Medium-voltage switchgear runs 52–80 weeks (Terrapin, June 2026).
- NYSERDA's own site-planning webinar put switchgear at 16–20 months.
- In Greece CSD (RG&E territory), the district needs about 5 MW for a full electric fleet. Its substation has 1.5 MW to spare.

**2. The clocks run out of step.** The five-year, 5,000-miles-a-year operating obligation starts at delivery. The charging voucher pays only after the chargers work, within 15 to 27 months. A bus parked while waiting for power burns its compliance window, and the district's charger money stays unpaid. NYSERDA's data shows 225 buses in projects approved more than a year ago that are still open, 150 of them for more than 18 months. Our [implementation gap report](/research/ny-implementation-gap) tracks this backlog.

**3. Utility-side costs fall on the district.** NYSBIP does not pay for utility transformers or line work. Make-ready covers up to 90% of the utility's standard design. NYSERDA warns that customers may have to pay for upgrades beyond it.

**4. Skipping the FEP.** Without a plan, a priority district loses $30,000 per bus in charging money and four or more buses of cap. It also misses the load study that tells it whether the depot has power at all. Start the FEP and the utility request together.

**5. Scrappage paperwork.** The scrappage bonus is paid only with a signed attestation from a dismantler. First-time scrappers must contact NYSERDA for an assigned scrap auditor before cutting.

**6. Missing the dealer deadlines.** Award letters are due in 4 months and the final purchase order in 9. If they are missed, the voucher can be cancelled and the place in the first-come queue lost.

## Where AXIVAI fits

NYSBIP pays for buses and chargers, but it cannot speed up a transformer, a substation upgrade or a utility queue. AXIVAI delivers battery power by truck into storage on the depot yard, so vouchered buses can run while the permanent grid connection is built. Battery storage connected to a charger is an eligible NYSBIP charging cost. AXIVAI is preparing its first pilot sites. See [off-grid fleet charging](/off-grid-fleet-charging).
