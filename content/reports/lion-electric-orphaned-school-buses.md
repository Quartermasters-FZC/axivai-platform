Lion Electric was one of the three big electric school bus makers in the United States, next to Blue Bird and Thomas Built (WRI, 2022). It took about $160 million in EPA Clean School Bus money directly, and districts spent more of their own awards on Lion buses. Its restructuring ended with a Quebec-only company. In July 2025 the court-appointed monitor told US customers that their warranties and purchase orders were void. This review sets out what happened, which districts were hit, who now keeps Lion buses running, and what buyers should write into their next contract.

## From creditor protection to a Quebec-only company

Lion's US wind-down started before the court filing. On 1 December 2024 the company laid off 400 workers and suspended production at its Joliet, Illinois, plant, leaving about 300 employees (Shaw Local). Payments to lenders were due on 16 December.

The deal was a reverse vesting order: the buyers took the operating company and its Saint-Jérôme plant, and the liabilities they did not want moved into a separate company to be wound up in bankruptcy. The Quebec government had refused to put more public money into Lion. The investors came back with a smaller bid once Quebec renewed its electric school bus subsidy at up to C$240,000 per bus, up from C$175,000.

The new company, renamed LION, builds electric school buses for Quebec. It dropped trucks. Groupe MACH's announcement promised to honour warranties on about 1,000 buses in the province and to restore parts and maintenance there gradually.

## What the sale left US customers

The monitor's July 2025 letter was blunt. School Transportation News quoted it: "all warranties and purchase orders in the United States were transferred into the new corporation to be bankrupted." That company has no operations, so a US district holding a Lion warranty holds a claim in a bankruptcy, not a service promise.

Three things went at once:

- **Warranties.** Lion's batteries and parts had carried eight-year warranties, against a typical 10-year bus life (Shaw Local). Districts now pay for every repair.
- **Open orders.** Undelivered buses will not be built. In a 7 February 2025 post, EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin put the undelivered total at $95 million across 55 districts, a figure first reported by the Washington Free Beacon from federal data.
- **The local relationship.** Herscher's superintendent told Shaw Local that the Lion representative who served the district was among those laid off in December 2024. Lion's US service network closed with the Joliet plant.

The EPA told STN it "is not able to offer legal advice to school districts regarding their warranty contracts with Lion Electric" and referred questions to the Justice Department.

## How many Lion buses are on US roads

No public register counts Lion buses by state, and published totals disagree. E&E News and The Energy Mix cited about 3,400 Lion buses sold in the US. That cannot be right. Lion's own third-quarter 2024 results reported "more than 2,200 vehicles on the road" across North America, buses and trucks combined. About 1,200 LionC buses run in Quebec, and other provinces have more (New Brunswick pulled 22 after the Montreal fire). The US fleet is therefore well under 1,000 vehicles of all types.

Lion's US sales had already slowed before the filing. In the first nine months of 2024 it delivered 92 vehicles in the US against 294 in Canada (Lion Q3 2024 results).

Where the US buses went is known only district by district. California was the first big market: First Priority GreenFleet, Twin Rivers Unified and Los Angeles Unified all ran Lion buses, and LAUSD says 28 of its 250 electric buses are Lions. New York's first electric school buses, in White Plains, were Lions; see our [White Plains V2G case study](/research/white-plains-v2g). Clean School Bus awards later spread Lion buses to Illinois, Maine, Michigan and Louisiana. In Maine, Lion buses went to nine of the 30 districts that received 72 electric buses through the EPA program, and they were the only make with persistent problems (Central Maine).

Two patterns stand out. First, small districts are hit hardest. Herscher has in-house mechanics and diagnostic software; Pembroke has one bus. Second, the EPA's scrappage rule removed the fallback: districts destroyed working diesel buses to receive the award, so an idle Lion bus means a missing route, not a spare in the yard.

Maine is a partial exception. Most Maine districts bought through the state's school bus purchasing program, and the Portland Press Herald reported in January 2026 that districts including Yarmouth, Vinalhaven and Winthrop no longer have to repay the EPA. Under the federal program, a district that stops using a grant-funded bus can owe back the roughly $400,000 that bought it.

## Parts, diagnostics and service after Lion

Lion buses are now serviced by third parties, district mechanics and, for parts, the Quebec company. The Electric School Bus Initiative at the World Resources Institute lists the options it knows of without endorsing them:

- **Fleet EForce** (Frederick, Colorado) announced nationwide Lion service on 1 October 2025: remote and on-site diagnostics, repairs, preventive maintenance and a Colorado parts warehouse stocked from Lion in Canada. It hired two former Lion technicians.
- **First Student** will do Lion warranty-type work for a fee and takes other EV maintenance case by case (STN).
- **Highland Electric Fleets** runs an aftermarket buyout and service program for out-of-service electric buses. Its page does not name Lion specifically.
- **CALSTART** runs an intake form and national forum for affected districts.

Safety work continues too. After the Montreal fire, LION issued a 15 September 2025 bulletin covering LionC Gen 1–3 electric buses and Lion360 diesel buses: a 30-minute inspection and, where needed, a four-hour repair to the 12-volt heating circuit. The traction battery was not involved. The Initiative says any qualified technician comfortable with 12V systems can do the work. Gen 4 buses, with a "5" as the eighth VIN character, are not affected. US owners should confirm this work is done, since no Lion dealer will call to schedule it.

## What it means for the Clean School Bus Program

Lion is now part of the federal argument over the program. On 19 February 2026 the EPA said it would not award the 2024 rebate round and would redesign the program for a 2026 grant round open to propane, CNG, LNG, biofuel and hydrogen as well as electric buses. Its notice says about $2.7 billion was awarded under the prior administration, roughly 90% of it to electric buses, and names Lion: about $160 million paid to the company directly, with some buses "undelivered and inadequately maintained." The agency says it will evaluate its options once the bankruptcy concludes.

For districts, three practical points follow:

- **Undelivered awards.** The Electric School Bus Initiative advises districts with Lion orders to ask the EPA about changing purchase orders, moving the award to another maker or drawing down funds.
- **Delivered buses.** Award terms still apply to buses a district owns. Maine's Education Commissioner wrote to the EPA in 2025 asking how districts with faulty Lion buses should proceed.
- **Future rounds.** The EPA says new awards will bring pre-award risk checks and tighter documentation for drawdowns. Expect more scrutiny of bus makers' finances, not less.

For the broader funding picture, see the [electric school bus state guides](/electric-school-buses).

## Lessons for districts buying electric buses

Lion's collapse turned the warranty into the weakest part of the deal. Districts can protect themselves in the contract.

1. **Treat the maker's balance sheet as a spec.** A warranty is only as good as the company behind it. Ask for audited financials and check for going-concern warnings in public filings. Lion's quarterly reports showed only 92 US deliveries in the first nine months of 2024, months before it filed.
2. **Get the service rights in writing.** Ask for diagnostic software licences, service manuals, parts lists and technician training that the district keeps if the maker fails. Herscher could keep buses running partly because it had its own mechanics and software.
3. **Ask for warranty backing.** A performance bond, an extended warranty from a third-party insurer, or a dealer that carries part of the obligation all survive a maker's bankruptcy better than a bare factory warranty.
4. **Write maintenance terms that outlast the sale.** LAUSD now writes long-term maintenance requirements into its bus contracts. Smaller districts can buy from makers or dealers with a service depot in the region.
5. **Don't put the whole fleet on one maker.** Herscher put half its fleet on one brand. Spreading orders across two makers costs a little in parts and training and caps the downside.
6. **Plan for the scrappage gap.** If the award forces you to scrap diesel buses, plan backup routes or rentals before the new buses arrive.
7. **Build the depot to outlast the bus.** Herscher installed 16 chargers at each of two garages. Chargers, switchgear and the utility connection last longer than any one bus order. Choose standard connectors and open charger-management protocols so the depot works with the next maker's buses. Many of these districts now need new buses from another maker on a new timeline, and some will wait again for depot power before those buses can run. AXIVAI brings battery power by truck into storage on the yard, so buses can charge before the grid connection or transformer upgrade arrives.
