In April 2026, 6 of Miami-Dade County's 69 Proterra electric buses were running (WLRN). Broward County has parked all 31 of its Proterras at a landfill. Capital Metro in Austin put 46 brand-new electric buses in storage for at least a year in November 2024. These are not one-off failures. The same few causes show up again and again, and buyers can plan around most of them.

## Proterra's collapse and the Phoenix warranty gap

Proterra filed for Chapter 11 in Delaware on 7 August 2023 (Proterra 8-K). Its transit bus business went to Phoenix Motor, which closed the purchase on 12 January 2024 (Metro Magazine). Phoenix took on the warranty obligations. Agencies soon found that a warranty is only worth the service network and parts supply behind it.

**Miami-Dade.** The county bought 69 Proterra buses plus chargers, paid for with nearly $24 million in state and federal grants and $37.5 million from its half-penny transportation surtax (WLRN). In February 2025, only 5 to 7 were in service on a given day (Miami Herald via GovTech). By April 2026 the figure was 6. The county terminated its contract with Phoenix that month, saying Phoenix had failed its warranty and service obligations, and it plans to ask the Federal Transit Administration to let it retire the buses before their required 12-year life without repaying federal funds (WLRN).

**Broward.** Broward contracted for 42 buses at $54 million, withheld payment on the last 11 and spent $31.4 million in the end, more than 83% of it federal (WLRN). In a Proterra bankruptcy filing, the county reported a breakdown every 600 miles, against about 4,500 miles for its diesel fleet. Its attorneys wrote that "Phoenix lacks the financial capability to perform on its contracts, especially the warranty provisions" (Miami Herald via GovTech). All 31 delivered buses are now parked and retired. Broward is buying 65 Gillig diesel buses and 65 New Flyer diesel hybrids instead (WLRN).

**Capital Metro.** In November 2024, Austin's transit agency shelved 46 new Proterra ZX5 buses for at least a year. It cited problems getting service and parts from Phoenix, and the slow installation of end-of-route chargers (KUT; Austin Monitor). Phoenix agreed to pause the warranties during storage. The agency's operations chief put it simply: "We're not going to be burning warranty." In July 2024, Capital Metro had already said the new buses it orders over the next two to three years would be diesel hybrids (KUT).

**Newark and Philadelphia.** The Port Authority parked its 36 Proterra shuttle buses at Newark Liberty in 2023, after three to four years of service, because replacement parts were no longer available. They were still for sale in December 2025 (NJ.com). SEPTA bought 25 Proterras in 2019 for $24 million and pulled them in February 2020 after cracks appeared in the bodies (Billy Penn). The buses stayed in storage during litigation. On 5 June 2025, a battery in one of them started a fire that damaged 40 decommissioned buses at the yard, 15 of them Proterras (6abc). The FTA then asked SEPTA how it stores retired electric buses and their batteries (Billy Penn).

## Lion Electric and the school bus fleets

Lion Electric obtained creditor protection in Quebec in December 2024 (Lion press release via Nasdaq). In July 2025, Deloitte, the restructuring firm handling the case, said warranties on Lion vehicles bought outside Quebec were "null and void" (Electrek). Winthrop Public Schools in Maine parked all four of its Lion buses. Herscher CUSD 2 in Illinois had 6 of its 25 Lion buses awaiting repairs. Yarmouth, Maine's two Lion buses did not run in the 2024–25 school year (Electrek).

For the full story, including what districts can do with orphaned buses, see [Lion Electric's orphaned school buses](/research/lion-electric-orphaned-school-buses).

Montgomery County Public Schools in Maryland shows a different failure mode. The district contracted with Highland Electric for 326 Thomas Built buses with a Proterra Powered drivetrain (ChargedEVs, 2021). The county inspector general found that buses failed to complete their routes 280 times between February 2022 and March 2024, repairs averaged 13 days, and deliveries ran late. In October 2023 the board approved 90 diesel buses for more than $14.7 million to cover the shortfall (WTOP). In December 2024 the district said it had recovered $1.5 million in fees from Highland (Bethesda Magazine).

## Recalls and defects: Burlington and Philadelphia

A recall can bench a healthy fleet overnight. Green Mountain Transit, which serves Burlington, Vermont, put five New Flyer XE40 buses into service in 2025 for about $8 million, roughly 90% of it from federal and Volkswagen settlement funds (The Autopian). In September 2025, New Flyer recalled 655 Xcelsior electric buses built from 2021 to 2025. Their Freudenberg (Xalt Energy) cells can short and catch fire when charged near full (The Autopian).

The interim rules strand buses in a Vermont winter. Operators must cap charge at 75%, park outdoors after charging, and not charge below 41°F (5°C). GMT sidelined the buses in November 2025. New Flyer is to replace the batteries within 18 to 24 months (The Autopian). By April 2026, three of the five had returned to the road. That same month, the federal government stopped funding the state's planned all-electric purchase under a $22.5 million 2023 Low-No grant for 17 buses. Vermont is now planning hybrids instead (VTDigger).

The lesson is not that electric buses fail in the cold. The cause is a defective cell, and the cold-weather limit comes from the recall remedy, not from the buses themselves. Philadelphia shows the other side of the risk: buses that sit for years still hold energy and still need managing.

## Waiting on chargers

Some electric buses are idle with nothing wrong with them. The bus arrives before the power does.

The EPA's Inspector General warned about this in December 2023. Bus makers told the OIG that delivery would take 6 to 12 months. One utility said building the grid upgrades for charging could take nine months to two years, with transformers, lines and switchgear the most common needs (EPA OIG 24-P-0012). If a district orders buses and power at the same time, the buses can win that race by a year or more.

Real cases follow that pattern. Concord, New Hampshire's three electric buses were not expected on the streets until 2026 because of a transformer shortage for its chargers (Concord Monitor, August 2024). In Austin, Capital Metro could run 40 of its 58 New Flyer buses only eight hours a day until more end-of-route chargers went in (KUT). Charger gaps were also one of the two reasons it shelved the 46 Proterras.

For how long grid connections take, and why, see [EV charging grid connection wait times](/research/ev-charging-grid-connection-wait-times).

## What an idle bus costs

An idle electric bus costs money in three ways.

**Capital that earns nothing.** The World Resources Institute, which runs the Electric School Bus Initiative, uses a 2024 MSRP of $401,000 for a new electric Type C school bus and $131,000 for diesel, with a 14-year life (WRI, January 2025). Spread over 14 years, the electric bus represents about $28,600 of capital per year, or roughly $2,400 for every month it sits. Transit buses cost more: Green Mountain Transit paid about $1.6 million each (The Autopian).

**Diesel that keeps burning.** When an electric bus sits, a diesel bus usually runs its route. WRI's parameters assume 14,000 miles a year and 6.59 miles per gallon for a diesel Type C. That is about 2,120 gallons a year. At the 2025 US average retail diesel price of $3.66 a gallon (EIA), it comes to about $7,800 a year per bus. WRI also puts diesel maintenance at $0.69 a mile against $0.35 for electric, roughly $4,800 more a year at 14,000 miles.

**The warranty clock.** If a warranty keeps running while a bus is parked, the buyer loses coverage it paid for. A bus parked for a year can lose a year of warranty. Capital Metro avoided this by getting Phoenix to pause its warranties during storage (KUT). Most buyers never ask. Federal funds add another clock: transit buses bought with FTA money carry a minimum service life, which is why Miami-Dade and Broward must seek FTA approval before retiring their buses early (WLRN).

## Lessons for buyers

1. **Check the maker can last the warranty.** Proterra and Lion were both market leaders when agencies bought from them. Ask for financial statements, a parts-supply plan and a named local service partner. Ask what happens to the warranty if the company is sold.
2. **Write a storage clause into the contract.** Make the warranty start at in-service, not delivery, or pause during any delay outside your control. Capital Metro got this after the fact. Get it before signing.
3. **Avoid single-source parts where you can.** Newark's buses died for lack of proprietary parts. Ask which parts only the maker can supply, and require escrow of diagnostic software and service manuals.
4. **Start the power request before the bus order.** Bus delivery takes 6 to 12 months; utility work can take two years (EPA OIG). File the service request first, and plan interim power for the gap.
5. **Plan for recalls.** Keep spare capacity, know whether you can charge outdoors, and budget for running backup vehicles for a year or more.
6. **Manage parked buses as energy assets.** Keep stored batteries at the charge level the maker specifies, store them away from other buses, and monitor them. SEPTA's yard fire started in a bus that had not run in years (6abc).
