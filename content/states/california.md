California has more electric school buses than any other state and a law that ends new diesel and gasoline bus purchases in 2035. It has spent more than $1.3 billion in state incentives to get there. Its weak point is power: a district can get a bus in months, but a PG&E circuit upgrade averages two and a half years. This page sets out the mandate, the fleet, the money, the utility programs, and the grid timelines a district should plan around.

## The mandate and its deadlines

Assembly Bill 579 (Ting) became Chapter 445, Statutes of 2023, when Governor Newsom signed it on 8 October 2023. Its core rule:

- **From 1 January 2035**, 100% of newly purchased or contracted school buses of a school district, county office of education or charter school must be zero-emission vehicles, "where feasible".
- The rule covers buses a district **contracts for**, not just buses it owns. Districts that hire a private operator are in scope.

The law gives two routes to more time:

| Route | Who qualifies | Length | Test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-time extension | Any local educational agency (LEA) | Up to 5 years | Zero-emission buses are infeasible because of **both** terrain and route constraints, and a daily planned route can't be served by zero-emission technology available in 2035 |
| Annual extensions, 2040–2045 | LEAs with total average daily attendance under 600 pupils, or where every county they run a school in has fewer than 10 people per square mile | One year at a time, until 1 January 2045 | Same terrain and route test, shown again each year |

The California Air Resources Board (CARB) decides extension requests, in consultation with the California Department of Education and the California Energy Commission (CEC). The test is about terrain and routes. A slow grid connection is not on the list.

AB 579 also lets districts sign longer contracts for zero-emission buses: up to 15 years for transportation service or bus leases (up from 5), and up to 20 years where the district can buy or cancel each year (up from 10). It extended these terms to county offices and charter schools. Longer terms let operators and leasing firms spread the cost of buses and chargers.

The Department of Finance opposed the bill and estimated it would need about $5 billion of Proposition 98 school funding over a decade. A 2024 Assembly analysis put the cost of replacing the publicly owned fleet at $5.5–6 billion at about $400,000 per bus, before charging.

In 2025, AB 1111 (Soria) proposed relief for small rural districts, including a later deadline and new grounds for exemption such as infrastructure delays. It passed the Assembly in June 2025 and was held on suspense in the Senate Appropriations Committee. CARB lists it as dead. The 2035 rule stands as enacted.

## The fleet today

| Measure | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| School buses in California | about 20,000 | Assembly Transportation Committee, April 2024 (citing LAO, 2022) |
| Owned by LEAs | about 15,500; the rest run by private contractors | Assembly Transportation Committee, April 2024 |
| Students who ride a school bus | just over 500,000 (9% of students) | Assembly Transportation Committee, April 2024 |
| Fuel mix | 55% diesel, 25% gasoline or bi-fuel, 14% CNG, 5% propane, 3% hybrid or electric | Assembly Transportation Committee, April 2024 |
| Buses more than 20 years old | more than 4,000 | Assembly Transportation Committee, April 2024 |
| Zero-emission buses funded / on the road, 2023 | 1,900 / 600 | CARB, via Assembly analysis |
| Zero-emission buses funded / in use, April 2025 | more than 2,300 / 1,100 | CARB, 23 April 2025 |
| LEAs that have bought at least one | more than 300 | CARB, 23 April 2025 |
| Electric school buses operating, June 2026 | 2,265, the most of any state (US total: more than 8,500) | WRI Electric School Bus Initiative, August 2026 |

The operating count has roughly doubled in 14 months, from 1,100 in April 2025 to 2,265 by June 2026, as the 1,000-bus state round and earlier awards reach the road. Even so, electric buses are around a tenth of a 20,000-bus fleet. Over 4,000 buses are more than 20 years old, so the replacement need is large well before 2035.

Some districts are far ahead. Twin Rivers Unified, north of Sacramento, reports 78 electric buses and 30 CNG buses. Los Angeles Unified approved 180 zero-emission buses in late 2023, about 15% of its fleet. Others are wary: in 2023, Modoc Joint Unified, in the state's far northeast, turned down a $2.3 million federal grant for electric buses.

## Funding programs

California pays for buses through CARB and for chargers through the CEC, with federal awards on top.

| Program | Amount | Dates | What it pays for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CEC School Bus Replacement Program | $75M for buses (SB 110), $14M for charging, $1M for training | Awards 2019; all deliveries due by 30 Sept 2022 | Battery-electric buses to replace the oldest diesel buses, plus chargers |
| Proposition 98 earmark (2022 budget) | $1.5B | 2022 | Zero-emission buses for LEAs |
| SB 114 (2023) revision | $500M in 2023–24: $375M to CARB for buses, $125M to CEC for charging | 2023–24; intent to repeat in 2024–25 and 2025–26 | Funds ZESBI |
| ZESBI (Zero-Emission School Bus and Infrastructure) | $500M awarded to 133 LEAs for 1,000 buses | Awarded 23 April 2025 | Up to $375,000 per bus and up to $95,000 per bus for charging |
| ZESBI top-up | $15M from the FY 2026–27 budget | August 2026 | ZESBI shown as closed; a reopening is announced but undated |
| HVIP (Clean Truck and Bus Vouchers) | 726 school bus vouchers worth $134.1M | 9 Sept 2025 – July 2026 | Point-of-sale discounts on zero-emission buses |
| EPA Clean School Bus, 2022 rebates | $68.2M, 177 buses, 21 districts and schools | October 2022 | Bus replacement and chargers |
| EPA Clean School Bus, 2023 grants | $88.0M, 234 buses, 5 applicants serving 8 districts | January 2024 | Bus replacement and chargers |
| EPA Clean School Bus, FY2023 rebates | $91.2M, 380 buses, 47 districts | May 2024 | Bus replacement and chargers |

Points that matter for planning:

- **Demand outran ZESBI.** Applications sought about $690 million against $500 million available. Only $500 million of the $1.5 billion earmark has gone out as ZESBI grants, and the next state round has $15 million so far.
- **HVIP is where school bus money moved in 2025–26.** School buses took 726 of 3,634 HVIP vouchers in the 11 months after the program reopened, second only to heavy-duty trucks by dollars. Only one of those used the Public School Bus Set-Aside.
- **The CEC program showed what buses cost.** Its 2019 contract prices ran from $271,389 for a Type A bus to $339,370 for a Type D with a wheelchair lift. Deliveries reached 139 buses by the end of 2021, and 39 districts had finished charging installs.
- **Federal money is on hold.** EPA's three rounds above brought about $247 million to California for roughly 790 buses. In February 2026, EPA said it is "actively reviewing and revamping" the Clean School Bus Program. Its request for information closed on 6 April 2026, with a new round promised later in 2026 and no date set.

## Utilities and make-ready programs

The three investor-owned utilities run fleet make-ready programs approved by the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC). The utility builds the wiring up to the charger, and school bus sites qualify for extra charger rebates.

| Utility | Program | Budget | Target | School bus terms | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PG&E | EV Fleet (approved as FleetReady) | $236.3M | at least 700 sites, 6,500 vehicles | Charger rebates up to 50% of cost for school bus sites; site incentives capped at 25 vehicles | CPUC D.18-05-040 summary; PG&E |
| SCE | Charge Ready Transport | $342.7M infrastructure ($356.4M with evaluation) | at least 870 sites, 8,490 vehicles | Charger rebates up to 50% of cost for school bus sites; EV rates with no demand charge for their first five years | CPUC D.18-05-040 summary |
| SDG&E | Power Your Drive for Fleets | $107M over 5 years | 300+ sites, 3,000+ vehicles | Extra rebate up to 50% of charger cost for school districts | SDG&E fact sheet |
| LADWP | Commercial EV Charger Rebate, medium/heavy-duty | up to $2M per premises | — | School buses qualify; one new eligible vehicle needed per charger rebated | LADWP terms, rev. March 2025 |
| SMUD | Fleet charging support | — | — | Helped fund Twin Rivers Unified's depot charging | Twin Rivers USD |

The biggest program in Northern California is now closed. PG&E says its EV Fleet program is "fully subscribed and is no longer accepting applications". A PG&E-territory district starting a depot today can't count on that money and should check PG&E's current offers with its account team.

Municipal utilities such as LADWP and SMUD set their own programs and sit outside the CPUC's energization targets described below. Districts in those territories need to ask their utility directly for its timelines.

For how make-ready money fits into a full depot project, see our report on [school bus depots](/research/school-bus-depots) and on [fleet charging hubs](/research/fleet-charging-hubs).

## Grid readiness and energization timelines

California sets binding targets for how fast its large utilities connect new load. Under SB 410 and AB 50 (2023), the CPUC adopted **Decision 24-09-020** on 12 September 2024 for PG&E, SCE and SDG&E. The clock starts when the customer asks for energization.

| Step | Average target | Maximum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EV infrastructure service (PG&E and SCE Rule 29, SDG&E Rule 45) | 182 calendar days | 335 calendar days |
| New or upgraded circuit | — | 684 calendar days |
| Substation upgrade | — | 1,021 calendar days |
| New substation | — | 3,242 calendar days (8.9 years) |

The targets cover only steps the utility controls. Customer, contractor and permit time sits outside them, and the utilities report it separately. PG&E's own filings show the gap:

| PG&E filing | Rule 29 projects energized | PG&E days (average) | End-to-end days (average) | Circuit upgrade | Substation upgrade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30 Sept 2025 (applications 31 Jan 2023 – 30 Jun 2025) | 86 | 113 | 508 | 871 days | 1,225 days |
| 1 Apr 2026 (applications 31 Jan 2023 – 31 Dec 2025) | 161 | 111 | **560** | **950 days** | **1,285 days** |

What the April 2026 filing shows:

- **PG&E meets its targets while sites wait 18 months.** It put 96.5% of 2025 Rule 29 projects under the maximum, yet the average EV project took 560 days from application to power. PG&E's share stayed near 111 days. The rest is design, permits, construction and customer steps.
- **The end-to-end average is rising**, from 508 to 560 days in six months.
- **Capacity upgrades already run past the CPUC maximums.** Circuit work averaged 950 days against a 684-day limit, and substation upgrades 1,285 days against 1,021. These figures cover all of PG&E's upstream capacity work completed since January 2023. PG&E finished no new substations in the period.
- **The averages count only finished projects.** Projects still waiting don't appear, so the true wait for open projects is likely longer.
- **Funding is at risk.** PG&E says the SB 410 funding that steadied its timelines "expires at the end of 2026".

SCE's April 2026 filing has less to show. Only two Rule 29/45 projects under the new rules were finished in 2025. Their utility time averaged 150 business days against a 125-business-day target, and SCE says it can't yet fully separate its own time from customer time.

For a school district, this sets the planning window. A bus ordered today can arrive within a year. If the depot needs a new service, plan on about 18 months. If it needs a circuit or substation upgrade, plan on 2.5–3.5 years.

## The main barriers

1. **Power arrives after the buses.** PG&E's 560-day end-to-end average and 950-day circuit average are longer than a typical bus order. AB 579's extension test covers terrain and routes, not grid delay, so a slow hookup doesn't buy a district more time after 2035.
2. **Funding has narrowed.** ZESBI is closed with $15 million queued, PG&E's EV Fleet program is full, EPA's program is being redesigned, and PG&E's SB 410 energization funding ends in 2026. Districts that missed the 2025 rounds face a thinner stack.
3. **The scale gap.** About 2,265 electric buses run today against a fleet of about 20,000, with over 4,000 buses more than 20 years old. At about $400,000 a bus, the state's own analysis prices the publicly owned fleet alone at $5.5–6 billion before chargers.
4. **Contracted service.** AB 579 covers contracted buses. Advanced Energy United told the Legislature that 30% of LEAs contract out transportation and were shut out of ZESBI because they don't own buses. The longer 15- and 20-year contract terms help, but operators still need depots with power.
5. **Rural routes and terrain.** Battery-electric school buses have ranges of 75–210 miles, depending on climate, terrain and driving (WRI, cited by the Assembly). Long routes and mountain grades are why the law has its frontier-district extensions, and why districts like Modoc have declined grants.
