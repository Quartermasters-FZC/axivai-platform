New York requires every school bus to be zero-emission by 2040. It has about 50,000 school buses to convert and $500 million in state bond money to help. It also has districts whose depots can't take more than one or two more electric buses until the utility rebuilds a line or a substation. This page sets out the deadlines, the fleet today, the money available, and what stands between a district and a working electric depot.

## The mandate and its deadlines

New York Education Law §3638 sets two deadlines for every school district, whether it owns its buses or contracts for them:

- **1 July 2032:** every new school bus a district buys or leases must be zero-emission.
- **1 July 2040:** every school bus a district operates must be zero-emission.

These dates are new. The 2022 law set **2027** for purchases and **2035** for full fleets. Lawmakers moved both dates back five years in the fiscal year 2027 state budget, passed late in May 2026. The statute on the NY Senate site carries a revision date of **29 May 2026**.

Lawmakers cited cost, bus availability, range on long upstate routes and a lack of charging infrastructure. Districts had also told them the grid near their depots wasn't ready.

**Extensions.** The Commissioner of Education may grant a district up to **two extensions of up to 24 months each** on the purchase deadline. The Commissioner weighs the district's effort to comply. A district must at least have engaged with NYSERDA to get a second extension. No extension is allowed where NYSERDA has found that the district can comply.

With both extensions, a district could keep buying diesel buses until mid-2036. It would still need an all-zero-emission fleet by 2040. A diesel bus bought in 2036 would have to retire after about four years.

**New York City** has its own law. Local Law 120 of 2021 requires all school buses in use under city contracts to be all-electric by **1 September 2035**. That deadline depends on the commercial availability and reliability of electric buses and on charging and depot infrastructure. The state change did not amend it.

**Status at 1 November 2026.** §3638 still shows the 2032 and 2040 dates. No further change has been enacted since the May 2026 budget.

## The fleet today

New York has roughly **50,000 school buses** (NYSERDA, cited by School Transportation News, September 2026). Fewer than 1% are electric.

| Measure | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| District-owned buses (438 reporting districts, excluding NYC) | 15,835 (2024–25) | NYSED, October 2025 |
| Zero-emission buses among them | 117, up from 41 a year earlier | NYSED, October 2025 |
| Zero-emission buses on order at those districts | 95 | NYSED, October 2025 |
| Chargers installed at district-owned depots | 145 (2024–25) | NYSED, October 2025 |
| Electric school buses registered statewide | over 350, in 54 districts (September 2026) | NYSERDA, via School Transportation News |
| NYC's share of the state's electric buses | about one-third | New York Times, September 2026 |
| Districts that own and run all or part of their fleet | 440 of 609 responding (72%) | NYSED, October 2025 |

**Contractors run a large share of the fleet.** NYSED's district counts leave out buses owned by bus companies. Five members of the New York School Bus Contractors Association reported running zero-emission buses in 2025, with 63 between them. Of 268 districts that contract for transport, only 7 said their contractor used a zero-emission bus on their routes.

**The leader is small and rural.** Evans-Brant (Lake Shore) Central School District, south of Buffalo, owns 23 zero-emission buses in a fleet of 49, about 47%. It received $7.9 million in federal awards and $2.53 million in state and local awards, and has 25 chargers working (NYSED).

**New York City is the biggest single fleet.** The city has about **10,500 school buses on almost 9,000 routes**. About 7,500 run routes and park overnight at contractor lots. About 2,000 are spares, and about 1,000 are "park-outs" that go home with the driver (PowerUp NYC, September 2023). The Department of Education buses about 145,000 students a day at a cost of nearly $2 billion a year (NYC Comptroller, December 2025).

## Funding programs

### NYSBIP: state bus and charger vouchers

The **New York School Bus Incentive Program (NYSBIP)**, run by NYSERDA, is the main source of state money. It draws on **$500 million** set aside for zero-emission school buses in the 2022 Clean Water, Clean Air and Green Jobs Environmental Bond Act. NYSERDA released it in stages: the first **$100 million** in September 2023 and another **$200 million** in July 2025. The fiscal year 2027 budget added **$100 million**, for **$600 million** in all (NYSERDA, via School Transportation News).

**Bus vouchers** lower the price the district pays for each new bus.

| Bus type | Base voucher (new bus) | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type D | $156,000 | 60% of incremental cost |
| Type C | $147,000 | 60% of incremental cost |
| Type A | $114,000 | 60% of incremental cost |
| Repowered Type C | $135,000 | 75% of cost |
| Repowered Type A | $105,000 | 75% of cost |

*Source: NYSERDA program figures as reported by School Transportation News, October 2023.*

Priority districts (high-need districts and those serving disadvantaged communities) get bonuses of $21,000 to $39,000 per bus. More bonuses apply for scrapping an old bus, vehicle-to-grid capability and wheelchair lifts. **Charger vouchers** run from **$25,000 to $65,000 per charger** (National Grid ESB overview, March 2026). NYSERDA also pays 50–100% of consultant costs for a district's **Fleet Electrification Plan**.

**What the vouchers have bought.** NYSERDA's public project data (refreshed 26 October 2026) shows:

| Measure | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Buses with an approved voucher | 461 (456 battery-electric, 5 hydrogen fuel cell) |
| School districts and BOCES | 79 (77 districts, 2 BOCES) |
| Total voucher value | $83.3 million |
| Buses in completed (closed) projects | 177 |
| Buses in open (approved) projects | 284 |
| Buses in priority districts | 322 (70%) |
| Buses run by a third-party operator | 254 (55%) |
| Largest single voucher | $265,250 |
| NYC School District | 126 buses, the most of any district |

AXIVAI's reading of the same dataset found that completed projects took a median of about **18 months** from approval to close. See [New York's electric school-bus gap](/research/ny-implementation-gap).

**Watch the operating rules.** A voucher-funded bus must stay in the fleet for five years and run at least 5,000 miles a year (NYSERDA purchaser and contractor agreements). Selling or retiring it early triggers a prorated repayment. A bus that sits idle waiting for depot power still owes those miles.

### EPA Clean School Bus awards to New York

Federal money has been a large part of New York's early fleet.

| Round | New York award | Buses | Recipients |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2022 rebates | about $18.35 million | 51 | Three NYC applicants |
| 2023 rebates | about $95.7 million | over 350 electric, 5 propane | 47 communities |
| 2023 grants (announced January 2024) | nearly $69 million | 200, with 206 chargers | NYCSBUS (100), J.P. Bus and Truck Repair (80), Evans-Brant (20) |
| Leonard Bus Sales grant | $6.625 million | 17 electric | Six districts |

*Sources: EPA news releases, 2022–2024.*

The federal pipeline has stalled. EPA cancelled its 2024 rebate round in February 2026 and ran a request for information that closed on 6 April 2026. As of 26 October 2026, EPA's program page says only that it will share details of a new grant round "later in 2026." The revised program is set to include natural gas, hydrogen and biofuel buses, not only electric ones. New York districts should not count on federal money for 2027 budgets.

### Utility make-ready programs

New York's investor-owned utilities run a **Medium- and Heavy-Duty Make-Ready Pilot** through the Joint Utilities of New York. It covers the wiring, transformers and equipment that bring power to chargers. It stacks with NYSBIP.

| Site location | Utility-side costs | Customer-side costs (private fleet site) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tier 1: in or partly within a disadvantaged community | up to 90% | up to 90%, capped per kW |
| Tier 2: within 2 miles of a disadvantaged community | up to 90% | up to 50%, capped per kW |
| Outside both | up to 90% | not eligible |

*Source: Joint Utilities of New York; Con Edison PowerReady MHD page.*

- **Participating utilities:** Con Edison, National Grid, NYSEG, RG&E, Central Hudson and Orange & Rockland. Per-kW caps differ by utility. Con Edison's are $882/kW (Tier 1) and $490/kW (Tier 2).
- **Recent changes:** a January 2026 PSC order opened the pilot to shared charging hubs and to fleets without a vehicle voucher.
- **Funding is first-come, first-served.** It runs until each utility's budget is allocated.
- **National Grid** adds a load-management program. It pays up to **60% of on-site energy storage** and up to 90% of load-management hardware and software paired with bus chargers. Its EV Phase-In Rate cuts demand charges for eligible depots.
- **PSEG Long Island**, which is not part of the Joint Utilities, runs its own Fleet Make Ready Program and free fleet advisory services.

Make-ready money is mostly a **refund**. Districts pay up front, which often means borrowing that voters must approve. Only 54 of 609 districts had held a zero-emission bus vote by 2025, and 38 passed (NYSED).

## Utilities and grid readiness

The money exists. The hard part for many districts is getting the power to the depot.

**How long it takes.** New York's utilities publish no step-by-step energization times. The clearest public record is a **15 June 2026 Department of Public Service staff report** on EV charger load interconnection. It summarizes what developers and fleets told the working group:

- Transportation electrification projects take longer than expected: "a few months upstate, a year or longer downstate."
- Fleets can be delivered in a few months, but upgrades and approvals often take six months or longer.
- Some load studies take up to 20 weeks.
- Some projects sat with construction finished for many months before energization.

Staff recommended flexible and phased connections, plus end-to-end timeline tracking on a public dashboard.

**Where the grid binds hardest.** For some depots the limit is the substation, not the service line. Those fixes take years.

| Place (utility) | What is on record | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RG&E Station 124 | School-bus charging makes up **63%** of the load requested; $33.2 million expansion, in service 2028 | NYSEG/RG&E petition, Nov 2024 |
| NYSEG substations (Ferndale, Kents Falls, Clarence, Whitney Point, others) | Each lists "possible school bus charging" as a driver; in service 2028–2031 | NYSEG/RG&E petition, Nov 2024 |
| NYSEG and RG&E queues | 52.4 MW and 27.3 MW of load waiting on these upgrades | NYSEG/RG&E petition, Nov 2024 |
| Statewide urgent upgrades | PSC approved 29 projects, $636.2 million, 642 MW, including a Con Edison expansion at Zerega Avenue for fleet electrification | PSC, 12 June 2025 |
| Greece CSD (RG&E) | Needs about 5 MW for 190 electric buses; its Latta Road substation has 1.5 MW to spare; waited almost 3 months for utility information | WHEC, January 2025 |
| Naples CSD (NYSEG) | Power for one more electric bus before NYSEG upgrades a transmission line; will eventually need 23 | School Transportation News; NYT, Sept 2026 |
| Gouverneur CSD | Can add two more buses; beyond that, a transformer, backup generator and wiring costing about $5 million, about 10% of its annual budget | NYT, Sept 2026 |
| Central Hudson territory | About 68 districts; if all charged at once, peak demand would rise about 13%; only two had asked, so the utility is holding off on upgrades | NYT, Sept 2026 |

Utilities build when they have firm requests. Districts need firm costs and dates before they can win a bond vote. Each side waits for the other.

**Transformers.** Equipment lead times add to the wait. A utility or developer ordering a transformer may wait **2 to 4 years** for delivery, against a few months in 2020. Large substation transformers have lead times of 80 to 210 weeks (federal National Infrastructure Advisory Council, June 2024). See AXIVAI's research on [school-bus depot power](/research/school-bus-depots).

**What works where power is tight.** Managed charging stretches a small service a long way. NYCSBUS runs 14 bus chargers at its Zerega Avenue yard in the Bronx under an **80 kW** site limit by scheduling every charger (The Mobility House, June 2024). In June 2026 Con Edison energized a **45-bus, 23-charger** depot for GVC off Zerega Avenue, with a new service, a new transformer and a **$450,000** make-ready incentive. About 2,000 of the city's school buses, roughly 20%, are dispatched from that two-mile corridor. Con Edison says it is prioritizing further upgrades on Zerega Avenue and in Hunts Point but has given no dates.

The [White Plains vehicle-to-grid pilot](/research/white-plains-v2g) showed buses can send power back. It doesn't help a depot that can't get enough power in.

## New York City's contracted-operator model

In New York City, the Department of Education doesn't run most buses. Its Office of Pupil Transportation plans routes and hands them to private operators. There are **48 vendors holding 99 contracts** (NYC Comptroller, December 2025). The operators own the buses and the lots. So the chargers, the utility service and the managed-charging software all sit on a vendor's site, not a school district's.

That changes who carries the power problem:

- **Contracts are short.** In November 2025 the city extended vendor contracts by three years, to **30 June 2028**. A vendor with three years left has little reason to pay for a permanent depot upgrade.
- **Space is scarce.** NYCSBUS, the city-backed nonprofit operator with over 900 buses, says its yard would need to be about **25% larger** to fit traditional bollard and in-ground chargers.
- **Park-outs need a different answer.** About 1,000 city buses park at drivers' homes. See [park-out charging](/research/park-out-charging).

NYC still holds the most state-voucher buses of any district (126) and two of the three 2023 EPA grants. AXIVAI's case study of two city districts, [NYC school buses: who holds the depot power problem](/research/nyc-district-depots), maps who controls each step.

## The main barriers

NYSED's 2025 survey asked districts to rate six barriers on a 1–6 scale. These are the districts that rated each one 6, "most challenging," out of 599 responses:

| Barrier | Districts rating it most challenging |
| --- | --- |
| Lack of external funding | 243 |
| Utility support upgrades | 224 |
| Facility or construction | 199 |
| Vehicle range or performance | 194 |
| Lack of voter support | 163 |
| Contractor or vendor compliance | 98 |

*Source: NYSED Zero Emission Busing Implementation Progress Report, October 2025.*

**1. Up-front cost and voter approval.** Districts must find the full cost first and wait for vouchers, aid and refunds. Only 54 districts had put electric buses to a public vote, and 38 passed.

**2. Grid capacity.** 142 districts (23%) say they will need an electric service upgrade. Another **406 (67%) haven't yet worked out their needs**. In August 2025, 344 districts had no completed Fleet Electrification Plan (NYSERDA data in the NYSED report). The grid problem is still surfacing as districts finish their plans.

**3. Depots and space.** 36 districts expect to expand their bus garage, and 25 need a new one. In the city, yards are crowded and rented.

**4. Winter range and reliability.** Naples moved some runs back to diesel because its buses couldn't make enough heat in sub-zero weather. Bethlehem's 10 electric buses were out of service an average of 66 days each in one school year, and its next order of nine buses was diesel (NYT, September 2026).

**5. Contractors.** Only 7 of 269 contracting districts require zero-emission buses in their contracts today. A district that signs a five-year contract just before 2032 can lock in four more years without one.

**6. Federal uncertainty.** EPA's program has paused new electric-bus rounds.

AXIVAI's [implementation gap review](/research/ny-implementation-gap) weighs how much of the slow start is power and how much is everything else.
