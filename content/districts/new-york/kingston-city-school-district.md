Kingston City School District spreads its busing across five contractors and 103 square miles of city streets and rural towns. It has no electric buses vouchered yet, but it has started a NYSERDA fleet plan. Its utility, Central Hudson, has said it is holding off on grid upgrades until districts ask for them. For statewide rules and funding, see [New York electric school buses](/electric-school-buses/new-york).

## District profile

- **Enrollment:** 5,693 students in 2024–25, down from 5,777 in 2023–24 and 5,859 in 2022–23 (NYSED). That is a 2.8% fall in two years.
- **Area:** 103 square miles, in NYSED's Hudson Valley labor market region.
- **Capital program:** in May 2023 voters approved a five-year, $162.5 million capital plan, 2,212 to 1,005. It is mostly air-conditioning and ventilation, plus windows, roofs, fire protection and parking. The plan uses $10 million from a capital reserve, and the rest is covered by state building aid of roughly 75%. Work runs school by school from 2024 to 2029. Robert R. Graves Elementary gets a new bus area (Hudson Valley One).

## Fleet and transportation

Kingston does not run its own buses. Its transportation office lists five bus companies serving district students:

- Ethan Allen
- First Student
- Birnie Bus
- Lezette Express
- Orange County Transit

The district coordinates routes and contracts. The companies own the vehicles, so electric buses for Kingston routes will be purchased by them.

With five companies, charging will be spread across several yards and several grid connections. Contractor capacity has been tested before: in September 2022, 9 of the 23 Kingston High School routes ran without buses in the first week of school, affecting about 250 students (Hudson Valley One).

Neighboring Rondout Valley shows how electrification arrives under this model. NYSERDA approved vouchers for six First Student electric buses there in January 2025, at $147,000 each, registered in Accord.

## Depot power and the grid

Central Hudson Gas & Electric serves about 309,000 electric customers in the Mid-Hudson Valley, including the City of Kingston (City of Kingston Common Council, 2024).

Central Hudson has described its position on school buses plainly:

- It estimated that peak demand on its system would rise about 13% if every district it serves bought electric buses and charged them at the same time.
- Only two of the roughly 68 school districts it serves had contacted it about electrifying their fleets.
- It is holding off on upgrades so it does not "overbuild our system early and cause our customers to have higher rates" (The New York Times, September 2026).

For Kingston, that means grid capacity at each contractor yard will be built on request, not in advance. The district has started the planning that leads to those requests. NYSED's October 2025 report lists its NYSERDA Fleet Electrification Plan as **underway**. In the Hudson Valley region, 56 of 101 districts (56%) had made no contact with NYSERDA.

Across 103 square miles, the plan should check winter range on the longest runs. See [New York's electric school-bus gap](/research/ny-implementation-gap) for how utility timelines compare with voucher deadlines.

## Funding

- **NYSBIP:** NYSERDA's project data, updated 26 October 2026, lists no vouchers for Kingston City School District.
- **Capital plan:** the $162.5 million program covers school buildings, not bus charging.
- **Contracts:** the price of electric buses and chargers will reach the district through contractor rates.

Program amounts and eligibility are on the [New York page](/electric-school-buses/new-york).

## Where AXIVAI fits

Kingston's electric buses will charge at contractor yards, and Central Hudson builds capacity when it is asked. If a yard's upgrade takes longer than bus delivery, AXIVAI can bring battery power by truck into storage on the yard, so electric buses can start on Kingston routes before the upgrade is finished. AXIVAI is preparing its first pilot sites.
